> Why Kings Show Up in Fall and Spring
> King mackerel, also called kingfish or kings, are a migrating fish. They follow water temperature up and down Florida's Gulf coast.
> In spring, they push north as the Gulf warms. In fall, they head back south as it cools.
> Twice a year, that migration carries them right past the mouth of Tampa Bay and the nearshore water off Pinellas and Manatee counties.
> The fall run lines up with a lot of other movement in the area. Bait is on the move, the mullet run is pushing schools toward the Gulf, and the first cool fronts start dropping water temperatures.
> When the bait and the temperature line up, kings move in close enough to reach without a long offshore run.
> Kingfish vs. Spanish Mackerel
> They share a family name, but they are different trips.
> Spanish mackerel are the smaller cousins. Our October report found them moving into Tampa Bay right on schedule, giving anglers steady, fun action inside the bay on an inshore trip.
> Kings are much bigger, much faster, and they usually stay outside the bay over nearshore structure and open water. Hooking one is a different experience.
> The easy way to tell them apart once they are in the boat: Spanish mackerel have bright yellow spots along their sides. Kings do not, and they have a lateral line that drops sharply about halfway down the body.

Huge schools of mullet move through the bay and toward the Gulf, and the fish that feed on them follow.
> What the Mullet Run Actually Is
> Every fall, striped mullet gather into big schools and start moving. They leave the backwaters and shallow bays and head out toward the Gulf, where they spawn.
> On Tampa Bay, you see it on the surface first. Dark patches of fish push along the shorelines, flipping and jumping, sometimes so thick the water looks like it is boiling.
> Mullet are not a fish most people chase on a charter. But nearly every predator in the bay eats them.
> So when that many mullet are on the move, the whole food chain moves with them. That is why this is one of the strongest stretches of the year for inshore fishing.
> The timing is driven by water temperature and shorter days, so it shifts a little from year to year.
> The first real cool spell of fall is usually what gets the schools moving in numbers.
> Why Snook and Redfish Turn On
> Snook and redfish are built to ambush bait along structure. During the run, the bait comes straight to them.
> Snook hold along mangrove shorelines, docks, and passes, waiting for mullet to swim past. Fall is also when snook start working their way back into the bay from the beaches, so more fish end up in the same water as the bait.
> Redfish feed along the flats, oyster bars, and mangrove tree lines. Our October report found them plentiful on the flats and along the mangroves, which is right where the mullet are moving.
> Speckled trout and Spanish mackerel are moving into the bay this time of year too, right on schedule according to our October report. Between all of them, the run rarely turns into a one species day.
> For snook, the timing lines up with the fall harvest window, open September 1 through November 30. Even if you are not keeping one, it is one of the best times of year to hook a good one.
> Why Tarpon and Sharks Show Up Too
> Bigger predators follow the bigger meals. Tarpon and sharks both key in on schools of mullet.
> Tarpon peak on Tampa Bay from May through August, but some fish stay in the area into the fall. Fish that are still around during the run often feed right in the middle of the schools.
> Sharks are even more reliable. Bull sharks, blacktips, and other species shadow the schools along the passes and channels, picking off mullet at the edges.
> Our September shark report found the smaller sharks very active, with warm water keeping them feeding close to the boat. As the mullet start pushing, that action has fuel behind it.
> That mix is what makes the run special. On a single inshore trip, the fish you hook might be a slot redfish one hour and something with a lot more shoulders the next.
> How a Mullet Run Trip Plays Out

#### When Is Snook Season in Tampa Bay?
https://www.charterfishingtampabay.com/post/snook-season-tampa-bay

> Charter Fishing Tampa Bay
> Oct 23
> 3 min read
> Quick Answer: Snook in Tampa Bay can be kept during two windows a year, September 1 through November 30 and March 1 through April 30, one fish per person inside a 28 to 33 inch slot (total length). Outside those windows the season is closed to harvest, but catch and release snook fishing stays open, and we run snook trips all year.
> When Snook Season Is Open in Tampa Bay
> Tampa Bay falls under Florida's Gulf snook rules, not the Atlantic rules. That matters, because the two coasts have different dates and different slot sizes.
> On the Gulf side, harvest opens twice: once in spring and once in fall. This fall's window runs September 1 through November 30, 2026. The next spring window runs March 1 through April 30, 2027.
> The closed months line up with the times snook need protection most. Winter cold is hard on them, and summer is their spawning season.
> If you are reading this in October, you are fishing the fall window right now.
> Fall is a good time to be on the water for snook. As the water starts to cool, snook that spent the summer around the beaches and passes begin working their way back into the bay, the rivers, and the backcountry.
> That move puts them along the mangroves and docks we fish every day. The fall mullet run adds to it, putting a lot of bait in front of hungry fish.
> By the time the fall window closes, the first cold fronts are usually pushing snook deeper into protected water for the winter.
> One more rule worth knowing: keeping a snook takes a snook permit on top of a saltwater fishing license. On our charters, both are covered for everyone on board, so there is nothing extra to buy before your trip.
> What You Can Keep, and What Goes Back
> During an open window, the limit is one snook per person. The fish has to measure between 28 and 33 inches total length, and snook can only be taken on hook and line. No nets, no spears, no shortcuts.
> That slot is narrow on purpose. Fish under it are young, and fish over it are the big breeding females that keep the bay full of snook.
> So even in season, plenty of the snook you catch go back. That is normal, and it is part of why Tampa Bay still has a healthy snook fishery.
> If keeping a fish matters to you, tell us when you book. We will plan the trip around the open window and the water most likely to hold slot fish.
> What a Closed Season Snook Trip Looks Like
> Closed season does not mean closed fishing. It means every snook goes back in the water.
> For a lot of our guests, that changes nothing. The fight is the reason people chase snook, not the fillet.
> A closed season trip still runs the same water. We fish mangrove shorelines, oyster bars, grass flats, docks, and passes, using live pilchards, shrimp, and artificial lures.
> The difference is how we handle the fish. Quick photos, a wet hand, and a fast release so the fish swims off strong.
> Snook are also one of several targets on an inshore day. Redfish and trout share the same flats, so a closed snook season rarely means a slow trip.
> What We Fish When Snook Are Out of Season
> If you would rather take fish home, the calendar gives you options in every closed month.
> In winter, sheepshead stack up on reefs, rock piles, docks, and bridge pilings. They are some of the best eating fish in the bay, with clean, mild fillets.
> Hogfish trips run from fall through winter and spring. Bottom fishing nearshore and offshore also stays strong through the cooler months for snapper, grouper (when in season), and sheepshead.
> Redfish can be caught any time of the year in Tampa Bay, in almost any weather, across the bays, marshes, rivers, and nearshore areas.
> In summer, while snook are closed to harvest, tarpon are the big draw. The peak tarpon window on Tampa Bay runs May through August.
